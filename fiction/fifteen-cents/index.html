Fifteen Cents

John, my brother, and I move pianos. We are Flett Brothers, of Stromness. In May last year Netta Moar rang up, wanting her Chappell upright carried from the front room up to the attic, and left there. We knew the upright. We'd carried it out in ninety-eight, with her floor up to get at the rot, and had it back in within the month, so we knew the weight of that piano. And we knew her attic stair as well. Twelve steps. It turns at the sixth, and the top half is nearly a ladder. John said no. She told him she'd pay to have that banister off the stair. "That's the least of it," John said. "Come and look it over, at any rate. There's tea, and I'll tell you why." So we went. John measured the stair, and I stayed and got the explaining. That is how I know the half of this. Netta tuned every piano in Stromness and the West Mainland for fifty years, and the school ones. She was known for coming back the day after, unasked, to fix a thing no one else had heard, and not charging them for the visit. She counts things. I drove her once to Finstown to look at a hall piano, and at the junction we waited behind a car that had its indicator going, and ours going. "Yours is eighty-eight to the minute," she said, "and his makes ninety-two. They were together just now. Give it fifteen seconds." I counted fifteen and the two lights flashed together and drifted apart, and were together again at thirty. She said that this was all tuning was. Two strings at nearly the same note do what those lights did: the sound swells and thins and swells again. A tuner calls those swells beats. You count them and turn the tuning pin till they beat at the rate you want. At tea she said her hearing has no bottom. "A note is one thing that happens over and over, fast. Slow it down, and it's a rhythm. Tap the table, Magnus. Three with your right hand while your left does two." It took me a while. "That's a fifth," she said. "A hundred times quicker, it's two notes, like the start of Twinkle Twinkle. For most folk it stops being a note at about twenty beats to the second. The biggest organ pipes do sixteen, and you feel that more than hear it. Under that they'd call it a rhythm. I can't say where one turns into the other." She held middle C down, unsounded, struck the C below, and let it go. The upper string was still singing, and nothing at all had touched it. "That's resonance," she said. "A string will take a push from anything that keeps its time." So she hears the swell as a note too low for anyone else, one wave every twenty seconds or so. When a gale blows south of Iceland the longest waves travel fastest and reach Orkney first. Shorter ones come in behind, and over the day the note rises an octave. By how fast it rises she can tell where the gale was. She keeps her freezer out in the shed, because its motor beat against the fridge's. Under the swell is the tide, twice a day, and under the tide are the two slow notes she has heard every day since she was born. "The first is the day," she said. "The day what?" "The earth turns once, and that's one beat. The other is the year, once round the sun, one beat. Play any beat twice as fast and you hear it an octave higher. Double the day twenty-four times and it's a G on the piano keyboard. The year takes thirty-two, and it's the C sharp below that. Both a shade flat." She played the two of them. "That's the start of The Simpsons," I said. "A tritone," she said. "The devil in music, the old monks called it. Six semitones, half an octave exactly, on a piano. The real one is six and a bit, and the day sits fifteen cents too high above the year." A cent, she said, is a hundredth of a semitone. "How did you measure it?" "By ear." "Is it loud?" "No. It's always there. I only mind it when I'm working." I said fifteen hundredths of a semitone did not seem much to mind. She said that it was her whole trade. "You'd think a tuner makes a piano true. She can't. Go up from the bottom C in true fifths, twelve of them, and you ought to land on the top C, seven octaves up. You land sharp, by almost a quarter of a semitone. The circle won't close. The old tuners left it all in one fifth nobody played. They called it the wolf. So I take a twelfth of that off every fifth, two cents apiece, and then the circle closes. Every key sounds a little off but none sounds bad to anyone else. That is equal temperament. Bach wrote the Forty-Eight for a tuning close to it. I set it all by counting beats, three slow beats in every four seconds on the first fifth, and so on. Every major third I ever left behind me is fourteen cents too wide. I know what the fourteen's for. It's the price of fifths near true: stack four of them and the third comes out wide." The day being fifteen cents out of tune she could not account for. She bore it till seventy-four, then wrote to the Observatory in Paris, who keep the earth's time, and asked what could be done. "And did they answer?" She had it in the tuning case, in with the forks. She copied it for me on the library photocopier.

Dear Mrs Moar,

Your letter was passed to me, as letters of this kind typically are. I have checked your figure and it is right. The year is 365.2422 days, which is 2 to the power 8.5127. That is eight whole octaves and 0.5127 of another, or 615 cents. An equal-tempered tritone is 600. May I ask how you measured it?

We cannot correct it. It is correcting itself, slowly. The Moon's tides brake the Earth's rotation. We know the rate from eclipses the Babylonians recorded on clay, which fall in the wrong cities unless each day is about 1.78 milliseconds longer than the same day a century before. The Moon moves 3.8 centimetres farther off each year as a result, which we measure by timing a laser against the mirrors the Apollo crews left there. An exact tritone needs 362.04 days to the year, which means a day 764 seconds longer than ours. At the present rate that is about 43 million years away. The interval should then stay within one cent of true for five or six million years, and after that it will be too narrow.

It has not always been a tritone. Professor Wells showed in 1963 that Devonian corals, nearly 400 million years old, laid down one fine line each day and one band each year, and they show about 400 days to the year. That is a minor sixth, 27 cents flat. A pure fifth is 384 days exactly, which the Earth must have passed somewhere between 200 and 300 million years ago. Some of my colleagues think the first day was about six hours long. That is a quarter of ours, exactly two octaves higher, so if they are right the day began on the same note it is on now.

You will know our difficulty from your own work. The year does not divide evenly by the day, so the calendar is tempered: a day is added every fourth year, and three are taken back every four hundred. Pope Gregory jumped forward ten days in October 1582 to bring the calendar back in sync. This office does the same for the clock. We have added 27 seconds since 1972. We do not make the clock agree with the Earth, but rather we move the disagreement to the last minute of June or December, which is given a sixty-first second, and few people notice that.

You ask what can be done. The day lengthens when mass is moved away from the Earth's axis, as a skater slows when she puts her arms out. Dr Chao at NASA has calculated that filling the Three Gorges reservoir in China added 0.06 microseconds. To finish the job you would need about 12.7 billion more reservoirs.

Yours sincerely,
J. Garnier

So that was why the attic. "He says twelve billion reservoirs," I said. "What's one piano?" "Next to nothing, and he can tell me exactly how much. Nothing I own weighs more, and up is the right direction for it. Up here, three metres up is a metre and a half outward from the axis. The rest runs along it and does nothing at all." John came down and said it could be done. The action, the hammers and their levers, would come out first, and the fall, the lid that covers the keys, and go up on their own, with the banister off, and a third man. "Mind the seventh step," she told him. "It's a quarter-inch higher than the rest. I've felt it forty years." John had spent an hour on that stair with a rule and missed it. He checked. She was right. We got Kenny from the garage to help. John went first, backwards, and called the time. He says "and," and on the next beat we lift and swing it a step and set it down. Then "and" again. On floors we break step, as soldiers do on a bridge, because a floor has a resonance too, and men in step find it. On a stair there's no choice. He's always a step above me, so whatever his feet do on a step, mine do on it a beat later. Netta, at the foot, said we were singing a round, like London's Burning: the second voice sings the first one's tune but starts a line late. At the sixth step we stood the piano on its end for the turn, and there it cost Kenny a thumbnail. From two till after seven. We put the action back in and she tried it and said it would do. Then she wanted the weight for Paris. "Two hundred and fifty kilos," John said, "all in." "How sure?" "Within ten." She wrote down two hundred and fifty kilos, give or take ten, then the three metres, which she had me measure twice over, and then the latitude, fifty-eight point nine six north. Garnier wrote back faster. She'd measured it by ear, she told him, and he let that pass. The piano, by his sums, had made the day longer by some two point seven yoctoseconds, give or take a tenth, a yoctosecond being a millionth of a millionth of a millionth of a millionth of a second. He had duly entered it in the record, which John took to mean she should stop writing. The tritone would now come into tune five picoseconds sooner, a picosecond being a millionth of a millionth, and would Mrs Moar kindly not bring it down? She showed that letter around town. The Orcadian ran it, page five, with a photo of her at the piano under the skylight, and then people rang us. We took the Linklaters' range up, a chest freezer, nine more pianos, one of them a pianola with the roll still in it, most of them not worth the carrying, each of them up a floor, a lift of three metres, or near it. The primary had a class carry all the hall chairs up the stairs on a Friday, and the janitor carried them back down on the Monday. A man from Kirkwall wrote in to the paper: he had carried his coal down to the cellar to cancel her out. "Would it?" I asked. "If he has enough coal," she said. I'm the one who keeps the job book, and we charge by weight and flight, so I had every weight. It came to four tonnes. Netta wrote to Paris at New Year, with the job book copied.

Dear Mrs Moar,

Stromness has lengthened the day by 44 yoctoseconds and brought the tritone forward by 77 picoseconds. I recorded the change myself.

Since you continue sending letters, you may as well hear more about this subject. What you hear is common enough in the motions and rotations of the planets. Jupiter's moon Io orbits four times while Europa orbits twice and Ganymede once, which is two octaves stacked, and the three are never in line on the same side of the planet. They have kept that up, like a round, since Galileo first saw them in 1610, and no doubt long before. Pluto goes round the Sun twice while Neptune goes three times, which is a fifth. Mercury turns three times on its axis in two of its years, a fifth with itself. The Moon turns once each time it orbits round us, a unison, which is why you have only ever seen one face of it.

Some of these resonances are held in sync by the self-correcting forces planets apply to each other. If a note drifts out of tune, gravity pushes it back with just a little more force. The Earth has no such force acting on its spin or orbit, hence the fifteen cents of dissonance.

If the gentleman in Kirkwall will send the weight of his coal and the depth of his cellar, I can record his contribution as well. After that, I hope you will not think me ungracious if I say that this record may be considered closed. The day length will not move by any amount we are able to measure, and there are only three of us in this office.

Yours sincerely,
J. Garnier

I count things now. The van's indicator blinks eighty-eight times a minute, as she said. The mains hum a G, a bit sharp, in every kitchen I carry an upright through. The ferry's engines beat against each other going out. I never heard that, or any of it, until she told me it was there. Now, even on the flat, John walks a step ahead of me, and I come in a beat late. So the piano stays. Bring it back down and the day loses what it gained. Attics are no place for pianos. It's cold under the slates and then warm, and the Chappell won't hold its tuning. She still plays, so she's up that stair with her tuning lever every few weeks. "Can you hear any difference?" I asked. "Don't be daft." Then she said the bass had gone flat again, and asked if I would hold the lamp.
